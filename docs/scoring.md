# Scoring

One spine, three paradigms. All three modules score against the same taxonomy of techniques (`taxonomy/techniques.json`) and the same four technique classes: ego, reciprocity, social pressure, and cognitive cue. Each module asks a different question, so each scores differently, but a class score always means the same thing wherever it appears: how much that class of manipulation showed up, weighted.

## Self-assessment: a susceptibility index

The self-assessment asks how a person would react, not what happened to them, and the web page frames it as a self-reflection prompt rather than a skills test: items are worded as concrete past-behavior frequency ("in the last year, when X happened, I did Y") rather than abstract belief, with balanced reverse-scoring so agreeing is not always the risk-indicating direction for a given technique class. The knowledge module below is the one that tests actual skill. Each answered Likert item (0 to 4, all five points labeled from strongly disagree to strongly agree) is tagged with a technique class or marked as an exposure item. A class's subscore is the plain mean of that class's answered items, already normalized to 0 through 1. Exposure items, covering public footprint, unverified contacts, conferences, travel, and reporting habits, average into a separate exposure score the same way.

The overall score is the class-weighted average of the four class subscores, combined in equal measure with the exposure score, then mapped to a tier with `tier_for`. Because items are already scored per person rather than per technique hit, `technique_weights` and `weighted_class_scores` do not apply here. The output is a vulnerability profile across four classes plus exposure, not a pass or fail.

The bank (`taxonomy/banks/self_assessment.json`) holds 94 items: 20 per technique class, split evenly ten forward and ten reverse, plus 14 exposure items. The web page shuffles it each session and draws a stratified subset of about 18: four items per technique class, split two forward and two reverse, plus two exposure items. The module scores over exactly that shown subset, not the full 94, so a retake draws different items and a different score is expected. The full bank and every drawn subset keep that same forward/reverse balance, so a constant answer lands the class scores near neutral rather than at a tier extreme. Answering all 2s, all 4s, or any other fixed pattern cannot game the score (see `tests/test_self_assess.py::test_constant_answers_cannot_game_the_score`). The page's scorecard shows the overall tier plus a per-class risk indicator (low, elevated, high) with countermeasure guidance for each of the four classes.

## Interaction assessment: an indicator rubric

The interaction module scores a conversation someone else already had, so it works closer to a detection than a survey. On the web page it renders as a yes/no/unsure questionnaire: each item shows the technique's name, a one-line description, and a concrete example, and asks whether it happened. Answering yes marks that indicator present, whether a specific technique hit or a cross-cutting conversational-control pattern from CDSE's own detection guidance (disproportionate interest, bridging toward a topic, circling back). Each indicator carries a `diagnosticity` weight. The overall score is the diagnosticity of the present indicators divided by the diagnosticity of the whole bank, so marking everything scores 1.0 and marking nothing scores 0.0.

Only the indicators that map to a specific technique feed the four class scores, through `weighted_class_scores`: pattern items and any technique outside the four base classes (composite, or one of the doctrinal-adjacent techniques carrying class `none`) count toward the overall likelihood but not toward a class bucket. When the overall score crosses `reporting_threshold`, the module sets `reporting_flag` and the result carries the list of matched technique ids.

The bank (`taxonomy/banks/interaction.json`) holds 34 items, 28 technique questions and 6 conversational-control patterns. The web page shows and scores the whole bank every time, no sampling, grouped into five collapsible sections (the four psychological classes plus conversational-control patterns) with a dropdown to jump between them. The scorecard puts the reporting flag first, as the headline risk indicator, ahead of the per-class breakdown.

The result here reads differently than a self-assessment score does. A high class score means several techniques exploiting that lever were marked as having happened in the conversation. That is a signal of a possible elicitation attempt. It does not mean the taker overshared or failed anything. The reporting-context selector on the page only matters once the reporting flag actually trips. When it does, the result names the channel to report to for the selected context: an FSO and counterintelligence office for cleared contractor or DoD, a security office for federal civilian, a manager or security team plus the FBI's IC3 for private industry, or the generic channel for general.

## Knowledge: graded drills

The knowledge module is a competency check, not a self-report. Each item is a scenario graded on three dimensions: recognition (did the taker name the technique), countermeasure (did they pick the taught response), and response (did they pick the reporting channel correct for their context). Each dimension scores 1.0 for the correct choice, 0.5 for a defined partial near-miss, or 0.0 otherwise. The response dimension resolves its correct answer from the item's `report_paths`, keyed by the taker's stated context (dod, federal, cleared_contractor, private_industry) and falling back to `general`.

Per-dimension scores average across all answered items. The overall score is the weighted average of the three dimension scores, using `knowledge_dimension_weights`. Each answered item also produces a technique-class hit (the mean of its three dimension scores) that rolls into the four class scores through `weighted_class_scores`, so a knowledge run yields both a competency profile and a class profile. Every item returns feedback text explaining the correct answer on all three dimensions, because the evidence favors practice with feedback over a one-shot quiz.

The bank (`taxonomy/banks/knowledge.json`) holds 30 scenarios. The web page draws about 9 at random each session and scores over exactly that subset. Within each shown item, the answer choices for each dimension are shuffled on every render, so the correct option is not predictably in the same slot. Scoring compares the chosen choice id against the correct id, so the shuffle changes nothing about grading. Combined with the random subset draw, that means the module cannot be gamed by answering in a fixed pattern. The page's scorecard shows the three-dimension competency tier (recognition, countermeasure, response), and per-item feedback that shows the taker's own choice for each dimension alongside whether it was correct, partial, or wrong.

## The weight philosophy

Three tiers of confidence sit behind the numbers in `config.py`.

**Reciprocity is evidence-anchored.** Its 1.5 class weight, the heaviest of the four, comes from Van der Kleij et al. (2023, N = 2,426), which found reciprocity, not authority, drove real disclosure of everyday personal information. This is the one weight in the default config backed by a specific effect size rather than expert judgment.

**Prevalence and subtlety are secondary.** A technique's `default_weight` in the taxonomy leans on how often it shows up across the corroborating sources (the FBI brochure, OSAC, NCSC, CDSE, DCSA, and Army FM 34-60) and how hard it is to notice in the moment. The two techniques appearing in only one source, Mirroring and Quote reported facts, carry medium confidence and a lower default weight so a deployment can drop them cleanly without touching the rest of the taxonomy.

**Everything else is a provisional default.** The other three class weights, both tier thresholds, the reporting threshold, and the three knowledge dimension weights are documented expert-judgment starting points, not measured values. `docs/validation-research-proposal.md` describes the study that would replace them with fitted numbers.

## Tiering

`tier_for` in `score/common.py` maps a 0-to-1 score to one of three labels using `config.tier_thresholds`: `high` at or above 0.7, `elevated` at or above 0.4, and `low` below that. The same function tiers all three modules' overall scores, so a tier means the same thing across self-assessment, interaction, and knowledge, even though the score underneath it is built differently in each.
