# Technique reference

This is the human-readable version of the taxonomy in `techniques.json`. It
covers the same four families, in the same order, and closes with the full
source list. The frame is defensive throughout. Every technique below exists
so a person can recognize it and hold a line against it, not so it can be
used.

Elicitation, defined in the doctrine's own words. FM 2-22.3 calls it "the
gaining of information through direct interaction with a human source where
the source is not aware of the specific purpose for the conversation," and
notes that of all the collection methods, it is the least obvious. CDSE
describes it as acquiring information from a person or group without
disclosing the intent of the interview or conversation. NCSC frames it as
intelligence gathered "through what appears to be normal, even mundane,
social or professional contact."

Elicitation is covert-purpose talk in ordinary surroundings. An
interrogation, by contrast, is overt, known-purpose questioning of a source
under some form of control. Catalog B below covers interrogation approaches
as adjacent material: they share elicitation's psychology, and two of them
(Pride and Ego-Up, Change of Scenery) bridge directly into it, but neither
is elicitation itself.

## Catalog A: Elicitation techniques

The conversational core of the tool. Each technique is grouped by the human
reflex it exploits, using CDSE's own four-class scheme: Ego, Reciprocity,
Social Pressure, and Cognitive Cue. This set is corroborated across the FBI
elicitation brochure, the State Department OSAC guide, the NCSC job aid, the
CDSE Accidental Conversations job aid (2025), the DCSA brochure (2021), and
Army FM 34-60. Two techniques, Mirroring and Quote reported facts, appear in
only one of those sources; they are marked medium confidence below and carry
a lower default scoring weight so a deployment can drop them cleanly.

**Flattery (A1, Ego).** Praise aimed at a person's competence, expertise, or
standing, offered to loosen their discretion before a follow-up question.
Watch for praise out of proportion to the setting, or being cast as the
expert. Acknowledge it professionally and do not expand; notice praise that
precedes a question.

**Provocation / feigned incredulity (A2, Ego).** Displaying deliberate
skepticism toward a claim so the target elaborates to prove it, or answers
out of irritation at being doubted. The cue is staged doubt that invites you
to defend a claim. Stay composed and do not prove capability with detail.

**Deliberate false statement (A3, Ego).** An intentionally incorrect
statement offered so the target supplies the accurate version in the act of
correcting it. Watch for an obvious error that seems set up for your
correction. Resist correcting it; ask a clarifying question instead.

**Criticism (A4, Ego).** Criticism of the target's work, employer, or a
rival, offered to provoke a defensive rebuttal that discloses detail. The
cue is an urge to defend what you are proud of. Recognize the ego-bait and
decline to defend with specifics.

**One-upper / can-you-top-this (A5, Ego).** A modest or exaggerated claim
offered first so the target feels a competitive pull to top it with a
larger, more specific one. Watch for that competitive pull to top a story or
a figure. Do not reciprocate the escalation.

**Feigned ignorance (A6, Social Pressure).** A pose of ignorance about a
subject the target knows well, meant to draw out an explanatory answer aimed
at a supposed novice. The cue is a pull to instruct a stranger who claims not
to know. Refer them to public documentation and do not fill the gap.

**Confidential bait (A7, Reciprocity).** A disclosure framed as
confidential, offered first so the target feels invited to trade a
confidence of their own. Watch for "just between us" openers. Do not match
confidences.

**Quid pro quo / volunteering / trading confidences (A8, Reciprocity).** An
offer of a fact, favor, or personal disclosure made first so the target
feels a social debt to reciprocate with information of their own. The cue is
an unsolicited disclosure tied to a follow-up ask. Recognize that the felt
debt is not real, and decline to trade.

**Good listener (A9, Reciprocity).** Sustained, warm attention and active
listening used to keep the target talking and steer the conversation toward
a topic of interest. Watch for unusual attentiveness paired with
topic-steering. Watch the conversation's momentum and limit work talk with
new contacts.

**Mutual interest (A10, Reciprocity).** A manufactured sense of shared
interest or background, built quickly so cooperation and disclosure feel
like a natural next step. The cue is fast rapport over a conveniently common
interest. Question whether the shared interest actually justifies
disclosure.

**Mirroring (A11, Reciprocity, medium confidence).** Matching the target's
body language, tone, and phrasing to build unconscious rapport that keeps
the conversation open. Watch for subtle imitation that lingers on one topic.
Note the synchrony and keep professional distance.

**Assumed knowledge (A12, Cognitive Cue).** Speech that presumes the
listener already knows the sensitive detail, so confirming or correcting it
feels like the natural response. The cue is a statement that assumes facts
you never shared. Verify what they actually know before responding, and do
not correct a false assumption.

**Bracketing (A13, Cognitive Cue).** A deliberately wide or wrong numeric
range offered so the target narrows it toward the true figure. Watch for a
wrong numeric range floated about your own work. Refuse to narrow the range
or confirm an estimate.

**Leading question / embedded presumption (A14, Cognitive Cue).** A question
built around a hidden presumption, so any direct answer accepts a fact the
target never actually confirmed. The cue is yes/no framing loaded with an
assumption. Reject the frame and answer on your own terms.

**Macro to micro (to macro) (A15, Cognitive Cue).** A conversation that
opens broad, narrows to a single sensitive detail, then widens out again so
the narrowing goes unnoticed. Watch for a conversation that narrows to one
detail and then eases off. Notice the funnel and redirect.

**Oblique reference / analogy (A16, Cognitive Cue).** Discussion of a
related or comparable system, meant to draw the target into using their own
work as an unwitting reference point. The cue is talk about an adjacent
topic that keeps circling back to yours. Do not elaborate, and avoid
comparative analysis of your own systems.

**Quote reported facts (A17, Cognitive Cue, medium confidence).** A citation
of already-public data, used as a springboard toward the unpublished detail
that would confirm or extend it. Watch for a line like "the press release
said X, so I assume Y?" Do not fill the gap past what is already public.

**Word repetition (A18, Cognitive Cue).** The target's own key word or
phrase, echoed back as a prompt for further elaboration on it. The cue is
your own phrase repeated back to you as a prompt. Notice the echo and stop
expanding.

**Direct question / survey (A19, Social Pressure).** A blunt, direct
question that relies on the social norm that a question posed deserves an
answer. Watch for immediate bluntness about specifics. Pause and judge
whether disclosure is appropriate.

**Questionnaires and surveys (A20, Social Pressure).** A survey or
questionnaire that buries sensitive questions among benign ones under a
plausible cover purpose. The cue is a survey mixing harmless items with work
specifics. Answer only the public items and refer the rest to the security
office.

**Ruse interview (A21, Social Pressure).** A fabricated interview, often
posing as a recruiter or headhunter, staged to justify probing questions
about recent work. Watch for an unsolicited recruiter pressing on recent
project detail. Verify identity and authorization through an independent
channel.

**Target associates / target the outsider (A22, Social Pressure).** An
approach routed through a relative or colleague, or questions about a group
the target does not belong to, to reach information indirectly. The cue is
questions routed through people close to you. Protect third-party
information.

**Exploiting the urge to complain (A23, Social Pressure).** An invitation to
vent about work frustration, used to surface setbacks, staffing gaps, and
delays the target would not otherwise volunteer. Watch for an invitation to
vent about work pressure. Do not discuss schedules, staffing, or resource
constraints with unauthorized people.

**Paper review / academic solicitation (A24, Social Pressure).** An
unsolicited request to review a paper or proposal, framed to draw the
target's sensitive expertise into the response. The cue is an unsolicited
review request that touches your own program. Consult the security office
before accepting, and release only authorized material.

**Offering assistance (A25, Reciprocity).** Genuine, unsolicited help
offered to a target, used to surface their interests and needs and to build
a sense of obligation. Watch for help that arrives with an interest in your
work attached. Recognize the obligation chain and report offered
inducements.

**Hypothetical situation (A26, Cognitive Cue).** A hypothetical scenario
built directly on something the target just said, inviting them to reason
through sensitive ground as if it were speculation. The cue is a "what if"
built on something you just said. Do not engage the hypothetical on
sensitive ground.

**Setting manipulation (A27, Social Pressure).** A deliberate shift of the
conversation to a relaxed, off-work setting, sometimes involving alcohol, to
lower the target's guard. Watch for a push toward private, informal,
unobservable settings. Treat off-site chats with the same guard as the
office.

**The Scharff technique (A28, Composite of Reciprocity and Cognitive Cue).**
A structured approach that pairs a friendly, non-confrontational tone with
the impression the interviewer already knows most of what is being
discussed, drawing confirmation or denial without ever asking directly. The
cue is a pleasant conversation where the other person seems to know most of
it already and never actually asks; you leave feeling you gave away nothing.
Recognizing the profile helps, but "they already know it" is a trap belief,
and awareness alone is a weak defense against this one. It is the technique
drawn from the academic literature rather than a federal brochure, and it is
also the most experimentally validated of the set (Granhag, Oleszkiewicz,
and colleagues). Rantamaki et al. (2020) found that simply telling people
about the Scharff counter-strategy did not reliably help them resist it,
which is why this tool trains a practiced response rather than a one-time
warning.

A cross-cutting detection signal, from CDSE, applies across the whole
family: watch the shape of a conversation, over its full length, rather than
a single line in it. Disproportionate interest in a single topic, deliberate
bridging toward it,
a high density of questions on the same subject, unusual persistence and
circling back, reformulation of a question you did not answer, and steering
that includes mirroring are all signs the conversation is not what it
appears to be.

## Catalog B: Interrogation approach techniques (adjacent)

From FM 2-22.3, Chapter 8. These eighteen approaches act on a detained or
knowing source, so none of them is elicitation, and each carries the
technique_class "none" in the taxonomy rather than one of the four
psychological classes. They share elicitation's underlying psychology,
though, so they inform the tool, and two of them (Pride and Ego-Up, Change
of Scenery) bridge directly into elicitation-style conversation. The manual
states there are eighteen approaches usable on any detainee, used in
combination except for the direct approach. Countermeasures here are a
defensive derivation from each approach's own stated doctrinal weakness, plus
AR 381-12 reporting doctrine, since the source manuals teach the approach and
not the resistance to it. Formal resistance doctrine lives in SERE training,
which was not part of this catalog's research pass.

**Direct (B1).** Plain, pertinent questions asked without pretext,
historically the single most effective approach, at 85 to 95 percent
effectiveness. There is no obligation to answer; declining is the counter.

**Incentive (B2).** A material or emotional reward, or the removal of a
negative, offered contingent on the source providing information. Refuse
the obligation chain, and report the inducement; this maps to AR 381-12
paragraph 3-1i.

**Emotional Love (B3).** An approach that directs the source's genuine love
of family, homeland, or comrades toward cooperating with the interviewer.
See the manufactured link between the emotional object and the disclosure
being asked for.

**Emotional Hate (B4).** An approach that builds on the source's genuine
hate or desire for revenge until it overrides their judgment about
disclosure. Recognize that the grievance is being weaponized to bypass
judgment.

**Fear-Up (B5).** A fear identified or created in the source, with its
removal offered in exchange for cooperation. Recognize the fear-then-relief
structure; doctrine itself notes that overshooting makes a source
unresponsive rather than more cooperative.

**Fear-Down (B6).** A reduction of an existing fear, positioning the
interviewer as a protector the source feels indebted to. Emotional relief
is not a reason to disclose.

**Pride and Ego-Up (B7).** Sustained flattery aimed at low self-esteem,
meant to draw the source into talking to earn credit or recognition. This
is the interrogation cousin of elicitation's flattery technique; the
drilldown into technical detail that follows the flattery is the tell.

**Pride and Ego-Down (B8).** A direct attack on the source's ego or
competence, meant to provoke disclosure offered in self-justification. Do
not take the bait to justify yourself.

**Futility (B9).** An argument that resistance is hopeless, meant to
convince the source that cooperation is the only remaining option. The
"everybody else already cooperated" claim is the signature of this
approach; futility alone rarely works without it.

**We Know All (B10).** An implication that the interviewer already knows
everything the source could offer, meant to make disclosure feel harmless.
A source who answers "then you don't need me" defeats it.

**File and Dossier (B11).** A padded or fabricated file referenced, but
never shown, to imply the interviewer already knows more than they do. This
depends on the source's naivete, and the same refusal logic as We Know All
applies.

**Establish Your Identity (B12).** A false insistence that the source is a
different, wanted person, meant to provoke an over-explanation offered to
clear their name. Proving your identity is itself the extraction vector, so
volunteer nothing beyond the identification actually required.

**Repetition (B13).** The same question and answer, repeated until boredom
or fatigue loosens the source into a more candid response. This is
ineffective against a source with self-control, and the repetition can
actually give the source time to regain composure.

**Rapid Fire (B14).** Overlapping, rapid questions meant to induce a
contradiction, which the interviewer then confronts the source with. The
trap is the urge to reconcile the contradiction on demand; slowing down and
declining defeats it.

**Silent (B15).** Deliberate, prolonged silence used as pressure to make
the source fill the gap with speech. Silence is a prompt, not an
obligation.

**Change of Scenery (B16).** A shift to an informal setting where the
questioning continues quietly under the appearance of a relaxed
conversation. This is the approach that bridges directly into elicitation;
the venue does not change what is actually sensitive.

**Mutt and Jeff (B17).** A harsh interviewer followed by a sympathetic one,
meant to make the source bond with and disclose to the second. The friendly
interviewer is running the same objective as the harsh one; doctrine itself
requires O-6 approval for this approach.

**False Flag (B18).** A false claim of nationality or affiliation, meant to
convince the source a friendlier third party is doing the questioning.
Verify who is actually asking; the misrepresented affiliation is the
manipulation itself.

One further approach, Separation, is restricted under Appendix M and is not
one of the eighteen; its text was not part of this catalog's research pass
and is not reproduced here.

## Catalog C: Social-engineering and cyber vectors (adjacent)

Elicitation and its enablers carried onto other channels. These are
delivery mechanisms rather than a psychological class, so each carries
technique_class "none." The two nearest neighbors to face-to-face
elicitation are pretexting and vishing, since both are conversational
extraction under a cover story.

**Pretexting (C1).** A fabricated scenario or identity constructed to
justify a request for information or access. The cue is a complete
backstory paired with pressure to skip verification. Verify identity
through an independently sourced channel, never through the contact details
given in the request itself.

**Phishing (C2).** Deceptive mass email or a lookalike site, built to
solicit data or an action from whoever falls for it. Watch for unsolicited
requests, lookalike domains, and manufactured urgency. Use phishing-resistant
MFA and filtering, verify links through a known number, and report it.

**Spear-phishing (C3).** Phishing personalized with open-source detail
about the target's role, so the message reads as legitimately informed. The
cue is a message that knows too much about your role and uses it. The
countermeasure is the same as phishing, plus reducing your own public
detail and out-of-band verification.

**Whaling (C4).** Spear-phishing aimed specifically at executives, using
urgency and confidentiality framing to route around normal process. Watch
for "urgent and confidential" requests that route around the usual process.
Executive impersonation controls and mandatory verification workflows are
the counter.

**Vishing (C5).** Voice-call social engineering, often using spoofed caller
ID, to extract information or codes over the phone. Watch for urgency by
phone, a discouraged callback, or a request for a verification code. Call
back on a known number, and confirm out-of-band for resets and transfers.

**Smishing (C6).** A text message carrying a malicious link or callback
number, disguised as an account or delivery alert. Do not click; verify
through the official app or a known number instead.

**Baiting (C7).** An object or offer, such as a dropped USB drive or a free
file, designed to bait curiosity into a compromising action. Watch for
found media or an offer that is "too good," including an unsolicited
exclusive file. Prohibit unknown USB media, block autorun, and train for
it.

**Quid pro quo, cyber (C8).** Unsolicited technical help offered in trade
for account access or data. Watch for an unsolicited IT-support offer
conditioned on access. Verify support contacts through the internal help
desk.

**Tailgating / piggybacking (C9).** Following an authorized person through
a controlled door without independently badging in. The cue is someone
without a badge following you or asking you to hold the door. Badge per
person, build a challenge culture, and escort visitors.

**Watering-hole (C10).** Compromise of a website the target group is known
to frequent, so a trusted destination becomes the delivery point. This is
hard to see directly; watch for an unexpected prompt on a normally trusted
industry site. Patching, browser isolation, EDR, and egress monitoring are
the counters.

**Business email compromise (C11).** A compromised or lookalike executive
or vendor email account, used to redirect a payment or sensitive data.
Watch for a last-minute change to wire instructions from an almost-correct
sender. Verify any payment change out-of-band, and report it to ic3.gov.

MITRE ATT&CK models the technical intrusion side of these vectors, so it
undercovers pure conversational elicitation; its closest node is T1566.004,
spearphishing voice. The ENISA Threat Landscape has the most complete
named-vector canvas, and it also lists honeytraps and scareware, which are
not broken out separately here.

## Catalog D: Collection methods and methods of contact (adjacent)

The vehicles a foreign intelligence entity uses to reach a target, inside
which elicitation runs. These carry technique_class "none" for the same
reason as Catalogs B and C: a method of contact is not itself a
psychological class. The CDSE Foreign Collection Methods job aid pairs each
method with a countermeasure and is the primary source for both fields
below; the DCSA annual Targeting U.S. Technologies report supplies the
frequency framing.

**Requests for information (D1).** An unsolicited request for information
arriving from an unknown or unverified, potentially foreign, source. Verify
identity and authorization before responding, do not respond if the request
cannot be verified, and report it.

**Solicitation and marketing of services (D2).** The placement of foreign
personnel inside a business relationship through a joint venture,
distributorship, or service offer. Review outgoing documents, give foreign
representatives stand-alone computers, limit sharing to the venture's
actual scope, and build a Technology Control Plan.

**Suspicious network activity (D3).** Hacking, malware, or phishing used
against an organization's network. This is the fastest-growing of the
collection methods in the CDSE data. Audit systems, do not rely on
firewalls alone, report intrusion attempts, and do not click unknown links.

**Academic solicitation (D4).** The use of students, professors, or
researchers to collect sensitive information under the cover of legitimate
academic activity. Treat unsolicited academic requests with suspicion,
respond only to verified individuals, and release only authorized
information.

**Foreign visits (D5).** Information gathering carried out during an
official or unofficial visit to a facility. Coordinate with DCSA before the
visit, brief hosts and escorts, walk the planned route in advance, and bar
recording devices.

**Conferences, conventions, and trade shows (D6).** The use of conferences,
conventions, and trade shows to link a targeted program to the people who
know it best. Brief travelers on how to respond to elicitation, display
mock-ups rather than real equipment, and debrief attendees afterward.

Elicitation and recruitment as a collection method is covered by Catalog A
and by the recruitment lifecycle, so it is not repeated here as a separate
entry. The DCSA report's own Methods of Operation list, for context, is led
by resume submission (the top method in its FY23 data), followed by
requests for information, exploitation of experts, exploitation of business
activities, cyber operations, attempted acquisition, exploitation of
relationships, supply chain, security protocols, and insider access. Its
Methods of Contact are led by email, then academic and professional
resumes, web-form submissions, and cyber operations. That report carries no
countermeasure section of its own, which is why it is paired here with the
CDSE job aid.
